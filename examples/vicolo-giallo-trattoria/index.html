      “Ask for the end of the bread.” — Rita

      Four ingredients, no shortcuts

      Rita Lombardi has a rule written in pencil on the inside of the kitchen door: if a dish needs more than four ingredients, it isn't Roman yet. Pecorino, pepper, pasta, the water it cooked in. That's the cacio e pepe that has kept this vicolo busy since 1989.

      Her son Marco runs the room. He knows the regulars by order rather than by name: “two carbonara and a quarter of Cesanese” has had the same corner table for eleven years.

      Tourists find the place by accident, usually lost on the way to the piazza. Most of them come back the next night.

      La storia

      Thirty-seven years, one kitchen

      From a six-table osteria with a borrowed stove to the most argued-about carbonara south of the river.

      In the spring of 1989, Rita and Bruno Lombardi signed a lease on a former wine shop with a yellow wall and a broken shutter. They had six tables, one gas stove borrowed from Bruno's brother and a menu Rita wrote on the back of a calendar.

      The menu is still short. Four primi, four secondi, whatever the market had on Tuesday. The carciofi come from Ladispoli in season and not at all out of it.

      “If the guanciale isn't crisp, it goes back. Even if I cooked it.” — Rita

      Bruno retired in 2015 and now holds court at the table by the door, where he tells everyone the stove was his idea. Marco took over the room the same year and added the pizza oven, which Rita still calls “the experiment.”

      The yellow wall has been repainted four times. It's always the same yellow.
